Power Supply

Introduction

A Power Supply Unit (PSU) is an important hardware component of a computer. It converts the electrical power received from an AC power source into the DC power required by the computer's internal components.

Components such as the motherboard, processor, storage drives, cooling fans, and graphics card need suitable DC voltages to operate. The power supply provides these voltages through different power connectors and distributes power to the connected components.

In most modern desktop computers, the commonly used power supply is a Switch Mode Power Supply (SMPS). It is designed to provide regulated DC output efficiently in a compact unit.

Operating Characteristics of a Power Supply

The operating characteristics of a power supply describe how it receives, converts, regulates, and delivers electrical power to a computer. These characteristics help determine whether a PSU is suitable and reliable for a particular system.

1. Input Voltage

Input voltage is the AC voltage supplied to the power supply from the mains electricity connection. Many modern desktop PSUs support a wide input range, commonly around 100–240 V AC, 50/60 Hz. The exact supported range is printed on the PSU label and should always be checked.

2. Output Voltage

The PSU converts AC input into several DC output voltage rails. Common output rails in an ATX computer power supply include:

Output Rail Common Use
+12 V Supplies power to components such as processor voltage-regulation circuits, graphics cards, drives, and fans through the appropriate connectors.
+5 V Supplies power to supported motherboard circuits, USB-related power circuits, and storage devices.
+3.3 V Supplies power to supported motherboard circuits and other components that require this voltage.
−12 V Provided for limited legacy and specific interface requirements in some ATX designs.
+5 V Standby (+5VSB) Provides standby power to supported motherboard functions when the computer is switched off but remains connected to AC power.

3. Power Rating (Wattage)

The power rating indicates the maximum output power that a PSU is designed to provide under its specified operating conditions. It is measured in watts (W). For example, a 550 W power supply is rated to provide up to 550 watts of combined output power within its specifications.

A PSU should be selected according to the power requirements of all installed components, with suitable allowance for system load and future upgrades.

4. Voltage Regulation

Voltage regulation is the ability of a power supply to keep its output voltages within specified limits when the input voltage or computer load changes. Stable output voltage helps protect components and supports reliable operation.

5. Efficiency

Efficiency describes how much of the electrical power drawn from the AC source is converted into usable DC output power. The remaining energy is mainly lost as heat. A more efficient PSU wastes less energy and generally produces less heat for the same output load.

Some power supplies carry an 80 PLUS efficiency certification. Different certification levels indicate different efficiency requirements under defined test conditions.

6. Cooling System

A power supply produces heat while converting electrical power. Many desktop PSUs use a built-in fan to move air through the unit and help keep internal components within their operating temperature range. Some models use fan-control modes that reduce fan speed or stop the fan under light loads.

7. Protection Features

Good-quality power supplies may include protection circuits to reduce the risk of damage during abnormal electrical conditions.

  • OVP (Over Voltage Protection): Protects against output voltage that rises above a defined limit.
  • UVP (Under Voltage Protection): Responds when an output voltage falls below a defined limit.
  • OCP (Over Current Protection): Protects against excessive current on a supported output rail.
  • OPP (Over Power Protection): Responds when the PSU is asked to deliver more power than its specified limit.
  • SCP (Short Circuit Protection): Helps protect the PSU when a short circuit occurs on an output.
  • OTP (Over Temperature Protection): Responds when internal temperature exceeds a defined safe limit.

8. Power Connectors

A PSU provides different connectors to supply power to computer components. Common connectors include the 24-pin motherboard connector, CPU power connector, PCIe or newer graphics-card power connectors, and SATA power connectors for storage devices.

Types of Power Supply

Computer power supplies can be classified according to their design, operation, and installation method. The following are common types and classifications used in computer hardware.

1. AT Power Supply

AT (Advanced Technology) Power Supply was used in older personal computers. It supplied power to the motherboard through separate connectors and commonly used a mechanical power switch connected directly to the power supply.

  • Used in older computer systems.
  • Had separate motherboard power connectors.
  • Usually used a direct mechanical power switch.
  • Has largely been replaced by ATX power supplies.

2. ATX Power Supply

ATX (Advanced Technology eXtended) Power Supply is the common standard used in many desktop computers. It provides standardized connectors and supports motherboard-controlled power functions, including soft power control and standby power.

  • Commonly uses a 24-pin motherboard power connector, though connector arrangements can vary by design.
  • Provides dedicated CPU power connectors and other component power connectors.
  • Supports standby power for compatible motherboard functions.
  • Used in many desktop PCs.

3. SMPS (Switch Mode Power Supply)

SMPS is a power supply design that uses high-frequency switching electronic circuits to convert and regulate electrical power. Most modern desktop ATX power supplies are based on SMPS technology.

  • Provides regulated DC output.
  • Usually has higher efficiency than older linear power supply designs.
  • Can provide several output voltages from one unit.
  • Offers a compact design for its power capacity.

4. Modular, Semi-Modular, and Non-Modular Power Supplies

Modern ATX power supplies are also classified according to whether their output cables can be detached.

  • Non-Modular PSU: All power cables are permanently attached to the unit.
  • Semi-Modular PSU: Essential cables are permanently attached, while selected additional cables can be connected when needed.
  • Fully Modular PSU: All output cables can be detached from the PSU, allowing the user to connect only the cables required by the system.

Power Supply Maintenance

Proper maintenance helps a power supply operate reliably and can reduce the risk of overheating or electrical problems. A PSU contains components that may retain dangerous electrical charge even after it has been disconnected from the mains supply.

Maintenance Guidelines

  • Keep the PSU Ventilation Clear: Make sure the air intake and exhaust openings are not blocked by walls, furniture, or other objects.
  • Remove External Dust: Keep the outside vents and surrounding area free from dust. Use safe cleaning methods recommended for computer equipment.
  • Check the Cooling Fan: Listen for unusual fan noise and watch for signs of overheating. Do not put fingers or objects inside the PSU.
  • Inspect Power Cables: Check external power cables and connectors for cuts, exposed wires, loose fittings, or burn marks.
  • Use a Suitable Power Source: Use a properly grounded outlet and a suitable surge protector or UPS where appropriate.
  • Avoid Overloading: Select a PSU with sufficient rated capacity for the computer's components.
  • Maintain Proper Airflow: Keep the computer case clean and ensure that other components do not block PSU ventilation.
  • Check for Warning Signs: Unusual smell, smoke, crackling sounds, repeated shutdowns, or visible damage may indicate a PSU fault.
  • Use Correct Replacement Cables: For modular PSUs, use only cables approved for the exact PSU model. Modular cables from different PSU models may have incompatible pin layouts.

Common Power Supply Problems and Basic Checks

Problem Possible Cause Basic Check
Computer does not turn on No mains power, loose power cable, switched-off PSU, or PSU fault. Check the wall outlet, power cable, PSU rear switch if present, and motherboard power connections.
Computer shuts down unexpectedly Overheating, unstable power, overloaded PSU, or another hardware fault. Check ventilation, system temperatures, PSU capacity, and other hardware connections.
Computer restarts under heavy load Insufficient or unstable power delivery, overheating, or a component fault. Check system power requirements, cooling, and PSU condition. Seek professional testing if needed.
Burning smell or unusual noise Damaged electrical components, fan problem, or overheating. Switch off the computer, disconnect it from mains power, and arrange professional inspection or replacement.
Storage drive or fan does not receive power Loose connector, damaged cable, or faulty PSU output. Switch off and unplug the computer before checking the relevant power connector and cable.

Basic External Inspection Procedure

  1. Shut down the computer properly.
  2. Switch off the PSU and disconnect the AC power cable from the wall outlet.
  3. Allow the system to cool before performing external inspection.
  4. Check the external power cable, plug, and PSU ventilation openings for visible damage or dust.
  5. Check that the PSU air vents are not blocked.
  6. Reconnect the equipment only when all external checks are complete and the cables are in good condition.
  7. If there are signs of internal damage, smoke, burning smell, or repeated electrical failure, do not continue using the PSU. Have it inspected or replaced by a qualified technician.

Important Safety Warning:
Never open a power supply unit for routine cleaning or repair. Internal capacitors can retain dangerous electrical charge even after the PSU is unplugged. Do not touch internal components. PSU internal repair should be performed only by a qualified technician.
Remember:
Keep the PSU vents clear, check external cables, provide proper airflow, and never open the PSU casing yourself.
