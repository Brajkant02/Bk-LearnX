Unit II: IoT Devices

1. Introduction to IoT Devices

An IoT device is a physical electronic device that can sense or interact with its environment, process information and exchange data with other devices or services through a communication network. Depending on its purpose, it may also perform actions automatically.

A typical IoT device may contain sensors, a microcontroller or processor, a communication module and a power source. Some devices also include actuators to control physical equipment.

Physical Environment → Sensor → Microcontroller → Communication Network → Cloud / Mobile App → Command → Actuator

IoT device architecture: sensing, processing, communication and action

Key Concept: A sensor collects information, the controller processes it, the communication module exchanges data, and an actuator performs a physical action when required.

2. Importance of Electronic Devices in IoT

Electronic devices form the physical foundation of an IoT system. They allow digital systems to observe real-world conditions and respond to them. Sensors, controllers, communication modules and actuators work together to make monitoring and automation possible.

Main Functions of Electronic Devices

  • Detect environmental conditions such as temperature, humidity, light, gas and movement.
  • Convert physical measurements into electrical signals or digital data.
  • Send collected information to a microcontroller, gateway or cloud platform.
  • Receive commands from a controller or application and operate connected equipment.
  • Enable remote monitoring, alerts and automatic control.
Practical Example: In a smart irrigation system, a soil-moisture sensor measures soil moisture. A controller evaluates the reading and switches a water pump through a suitable relay or motor-driver circuit.

3. Breadboard and Its Internal Connections

A breadboard is a reusable, solderless prototyping board used to build and test electronic circuits. Components and jumper wires can be inserted into its holes, allowing a circuit to be changed without soldering. It is widely used in IoT practical work with LEDs, resistors, sensors and microcontrollers.

The holes are connected internally by metal clips in a fixed pattern. Understanding this pattern is essential because two nearby holes are not necessarily electrically connected.

Breadboard internal connections and terminal groups

Power Rails

The long rows along the sides of many breadboards are called power rails. They are commonly marked with red and blue or black lines. The red rail is often used for the positive supply (VCC), while the blue or black rail is often used for ground (GND). Some boards split a rail into separate sections, so check continuity before use.

Terminal Strips

The central area is usually divided into groups of five connected holes. On a standard breadboard, five holes on one side of a row share an electrical connection. The group on the opposite side of the centre gap is separate.

Centre Gap

The centre gap separates the two terminal-strip halves. Integrated circuits are commonly placed across this gap so that pins on opposite sides do not become shorted together.

Important Connection Rules

  • Holes within the same connected five-hole group share one electrical node.
  • The centre gap separates the left and right terminal groups.
  • Power rails may be split; verify their connection before supplying power.
  • Never connect positive and ground rails directly, as this creates a short circuit.
  • Place a dual-inline package IC across the centre gap so its pins occupy separate rows.

Breadboard Parts Table

Part Purpose
Power Rail Distributes the positive supply and ground along the board.
Terminal Area Connects component leads and jumper wires in groups.
Centre Gap Provides space to place ICs without joining opposite pins.
Metal Clips Create the hidden electrical connections beneath the holes.
Practical Analogy: A breadboard is a temporary wiring board used to assemble and test a circuit before making a permanent version.
Remember: Breadboard holes are connected internally in a specific pattern; always understand the pattern before wiring.

4. Resistor and Current Limiting

A resistor is a passive electronic component that opposes the flow of electric current. Resistance is measured in ohms (Ω). In IoT circuits, resistors are used to limit current, divide voltage, set input states and support sensor interfaces.

A resistor used in an electronic circuit

Why Is a Resistor Important with an LED?

An LED can be damaged if more than its permitted current flows through it. A series resistor limits the current to a safe value. The resistor value should be selected using the supply voltage, LED forward voltage and desired current.

Ohm's Law

V = I × R
I = V / R
R = V / I

Here, V represents voltage in volts, I represents current in amperes and R represents resistance in ohms.

Simple LED Resistor Calculation

Suppose a 5 V supply is used with an LED having a forward voltage of 2 V. If the target current is 20 mA (0.02 A), the resistor is calculated as follows:

R = (Supply voltage − LED forward voltage) / Current
R = (5 V − 2 V) / 0.02 A
R = 3 / 0.02
R = 150 Ω

A standard value such as 220 Ω may be selected to keep the current lower. Always check the LED and controller specifications before selecting a resistor.

Resistor Uses

Use Explanation
Current Limiting Protects LEDs and other components from excessive current.
Voltage Divider Produces a lower voltage from a higher voltage using resistors.
Pull-up / Pull-down Keeps a digital input at a defined HIGH or LOW state.
Sensor Circuit Helps create measurement circuits, for example with an LDR.

Common Resistor Values

  • 220 Ω and 330 Ω: commonly used for LED current limiting, depending on the circuit.
  • 1 kΩ: often used in signal and general-purpose circuits.
  • 10 kΩ: commonly used for pull-up, pull-down and sensor-divider circuits.
Remember: A current-limiting resistor is normally connected in series with an LED. The correct value depends on the circuit voltage and LED specifications.

5. LED and Its Connections

LED stands for Light Emitting Diode. It is a semiconductor device that emits light when current flows through it in the forward direction. LEDs are used in IoT projects as status indicators, warning lights and simple output devices.

Basic LED connection on a breadboard

LED Polarity

LED Part Meaning
Long Lead Anode, usually connected towards the positive side through a resistor.
Short Lead Cathode, usually connected towards ground in a basic circuit.
Flat Side Often indicates the cathode side; verify the component marking when uncertain.

Basic LED Connection

Microcontroller output pin → Series resistor → LED anode
LED cathode → GND

When the output pin is set HIGH in a suitable active-high circuit, current flows through the resistor and LED to ground, causing the LED to glow.

Arduino-Style Code

const int ledPin = 13;

void setup() {
  pinMode(ledPin, OUTPUT);
}

void loop() {
  digitalWrite(ledPin, HIGH);
  delay(1000);

  digitalWrite(ledPin, LOW);
  delay(1000);
}

Common Mistakes

  • Connecting the LED with reversed polarity.
  • Leaving out the series current-limiting resistor.
  • Placing a component in the wrong breadboard row.
  • Forgetting to connect the controller ground to circuit ground.
  • Driving a high-current load directly from a microcontroller pin.
Practical Example: Indicator LEDs on a router show states such as power, internet connectivity and network activity.
Remember: An LED is a diode, so its polarity matters. Use a suitable series resistor.

6. Tri-Color LED / RGB LED

A Tri-color LED or RGB LED contains red, green and blue LED elements in a single package. By controlling the brightness of these three colour channels, the device can produce many different colours. RGB LEDs are useful for displaying system states in IoT projects.

RGB LED pins and connection

Types of RGB LED

Type Meaning
Common Cathode The cathodes of the three LED elements are joined to a common pin, usually connected to GND.
Common Anode The anodes of the three LED elements are joined to a common pin, usually connected to the positive supply.

RGB LED Pins

  • Red channel pin
  • Common pin
  • Green channel pin
  • Blue channel pin

The physical pin order varies between RGB LED models. Check the component datasheet or identify the pins before wiring the circuit.

Common Cathode Connection

Common pin → GND
Red pin → Series resistor → PWM-capable output
Green pin → Series resistor → PWM-capable output
Blue pin → Series resistor → PWM-capable output

Use a separate current-limiting resistor for each colour channel. A PWM-capable output can vary the brightness of each channel.

Arduino-Style Code

const int redPin = 9;
const int greenPin = 10;
const int bluePin = 11;

void setup() {
  pinMode(redPin, OUTPUT);
  pinMode(greenPin, OUTPUT);
  pinMode(bluePin, OUTPUT);
}

void loop() {
  analogWrite(redPin, 255);   // Red on
  analogWrite(greenPin, 0);
  analogWrite(bluePin, 0);
}

Uses

Colour Possible Status Meaning
Green System operating normally or connected.
Red Error, warning or critical alert.
Blue Connecting, pairing or processing.
Yellow Attention or a non-critical warning.
Remember: Use an individual current-limiting resistor for each RGB colour channel. Common-anode and common-cathode LEDs require different control logic.

7. Seven-Segment Display on a Breadboard

A seven-segment display is an electronic display used mainly to show decimal digits. It contains seven LED segments labelled a, b, c, d, e, f and g. By turning selected segments on or off, the display can represent digits from 0 to 9.

Seven-segment display and its segment arrangement

Segments

   a
 f   b
   g
 e   c
   d

Types

Type Meaning
Common Anode All segment anodes share a common positive connection. A segment is typically turned on by driving its cathode LOW through the appropriate circuit.
Common Cathode All segment cathodes share a common ground connection. A segment is typically turned on by driving its anode HIGH through the appropriate circuit.

Displaying Digit 0

To display 0, turn on segments a, b, c, d, e and f. Keep segment g off.

Displaying Digit 1

To display 1, turn on segments b and c. Keep the remaining segments off.

Connection Rules

  • Use a current-limiting resistor for each segment or an appropriate driver circuit.
  • Connect the common pin according to the display type.
  • Connect segment pins to suitable microcontroller outputs or a display driver.
  • Identify whether the display is common-anode or common-cathode before writing control code.

Practical Example: Digital clocks, counters, measuring instruments and lift-floor indicators commonly use seven-segment displays.
Remember: A seven-segment display is made from seven individually controlled LED segments; some modules also include a decimal point.

Pull-Up and Pull-Down Resistors

A digital input pin can become floating when it is not connected to a definite logic level. A floating input may produce unpredictable HIGH and LOW readings. Pull-up and pull-down resistors provide a default logic state.

Pull-Up Resistor

A pull-up resistor connects an input to the positive logic supply through a resistor. It keeps the input HIGH when the switch is open. Pressing a button wired to ground can then make the input LOW. Many microcontrollers also provide an internal pull-up resistor.

pinMode(buttonPin, INPUT_PULLUP);

With INPUT_PULLUP, button logic is commonly active-low: a pressed button reads LOW.

Pull-Down Resistor

A pull-down resistor connects an input to ground through a resistor. It keeps the input LOW when the switch is open. A switch connected to the positive logic supply can make the input HIGH when pressed.

Difference

Basis Pull-Up Pull-Down
Default State HIGH LOW
Connection Between input and positive logic supply Between input and GND
Typical Switch Action Button may pull input LOW Button may drive input HIGH
Purpose Prevents a floating input Prevents a floating input

Why Important in IoT?

Buttons, switches, reed sensors and other digital inputs must have stable logic levels. A floating input can generate false triggers and cause an automation system to perform an unintended action.

Remember: A pull-up resistor establishes a default HIGH state, while a pull-down resistor establishes a default LOW state.

13. Analog and Digital Sensors

Sensor outputs are commonly described as analog or digital. An analog output varies continuously over a range. A digital output represents information using discrete states or digital data. A controller reads the output through an appropriate input pin or communication interface.

Comparison of analog and digital sensor signals

Analog Sensor

An analog sensor produces a continuously varying signal, often a voltage or resistance-related measurement. For example, an analog temperature sensor may produce a different voltage as the measured temperature changes. A microcontroller with an Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC) can convert the voltage into a numerical reading.

Digital Sensor

A digital sensor provides discrete logic states or digital data. A simple digital motion module may output HIGH when motion is detected and LOW otherwise. More advanced digital sensors can send measured values through interfaces such as I²C, SPI or a one-wire bus.

Difference Table

Basis Analog Sensor Digital Sensor
Output Continuously varying signal Discrete logic state or digital data
Examples LDR in a voltage divider, analog temperature sensor Digital motion module, digital temperature sensor
Reading Analog input and ADC, where required Digital input or communication interface
Resolution Depends on sensor behaviour and ADC resolution Depends on internal conversion and data format

Arduino-Style Example

// Read an analog sensor connected to A0
int sensorValue = analogRead(A0);

// Read a digital sensor connected to pin 2
int motionState = digitalRead(2);
Meaning: Analog signals vary continuously; digital outputs represent discrete states or encoded digital measurements.
Remember: Analog sensors provide continuous output, while digital sensors provide discrete output or digital data.

14. Active and Passive Sensors

Sensors can be grouped as active or passive according to how they interact with energy. In this classification, an active sensor emits energy or a probing signal and measures its response. A passive sensor detects energy or changes that are already present in the environment. Terminology can vary across engineering fields, so the operating principle of the specific sensor should be checked.

Active and passive sensing examples

Active Sensors

An active sensor sends out energy, such as sound, light or radio waves, and measures the returned or changed signal. An ultrasonic distance sensor is a common example: it emits an ultrasonic pulse and measures the time taken for the echo to return.

Passive Sensors

A passive sensor detects naturally available energy or a property of the environment without transmitting a probing signal. An LDR, for example, changes its resistance according to the light falling on it. A thermocouple generates a voltage related to a temperature difference.

Basis Active Sensor Passive Sensor
Operating Principle Emits energy or a probing signal and measures its response. Detects naturally available energy or an environmental property.
Examples Ultrasonic ranging sensor, radar sensor LDR, thermocouple
Use Distance or object detection Light or temperature measurement
Practical Example: A car-parking ultrasonic sensor sends sound pulses and detects their echoes to estimate the distance to an obstacle.

16. Sensor vs Actuator

Sensors and actuators are complementary parts of an IoT system, but they perform opposite roles. A sensor transfers information from the physical world into the system. An actuator transfers a control decision from the system to the physical world.

Sensor input and actuator output flow

Signal Direction Physical world → IoT system IoT system → Physical world

Combined Example

In an automatic street-light system, an LDR measures ambient light. When the surroundings become dark, the controller switches on the lamp through a relay or suitable driver. The LDR is the sensor and the lamp-control circuit is the actuator output.

17. Signal Conditioning

Signal conditioning is the process of modifying a sensor signal so that it can be measured or processed reliably by the next stage of a circuit. A raw sensor signal may be weak, noisy, outside the input range of a controller or unsuitable for direct measurement.

Typical signal-conditioning stages between a sensor and controller

Why Signal Conditioning Is Needed

  • Amplify a weak sensor signal so it can be measured.
  • Reduce unwanted electrical noise and interference.
  • Adjust a signal to a suitable voltage range.
  • Convert an analog signal into digital data when an ADC is used.
  • Improve measurement stability and protect connected equipment.

Common Signal Conditioning Operations

Operation Meaning
Amplification Increases the amplitude of a weak signal.
Filtering Reduces unwanted noise or selected frequency components.
Level Shifting Moves a signal into a suitable voltage range.
ADC Converts an analog voltage into a digital number.
Isolation Provides electrical separation between circuit sections for safety or noise reduction.

Example

An analog temperature sensor may produce a voltage that changes with temperature. The controller ADC samples this voltage and converts it into a digital value. The program then applies the sensor conversion formula to calculate temperature.

Remember: Signal conditioning prepares a sensor signal for accurate and reliable measurement.

18. Sensor Calibration

Sensor calibration is the process of comparing a sensor's readings with a known reference and determining whether a correction is required. Sensor readings may differ from the true value because of manufacturing tolerances, ageing, environmental conditions, installation or electronic noise.

Need for Calibration

  • Improve measurement accuracy.
  • Identify and reduce measurement errors.
  • Make IoT readings more consistent and reliable.
  • Support dependable monitoring in industrial, environmental and healthcare applications.

Example

Suppose a temperature sensor reads 32 °C when a trusted reference thermometer indicates 30 °C under the same conditions. A correction may be applied if the error is stable and the calibration method supports that correction. For accuracy across a range, the sensor should be checked at multiple reference points.

Calibration Types

Type Meaning
Zero Calibration Adjusts the sensor reading at or near the zero or reference point.
Span Calibration Adjusts the response across a measurement range, often using a known upper reference point.
Software Calibration Applies a correction factor, offset or calibration curve in software.
Hardware Calibration Adjusts circuit components or hardware settings to correct the measurement.
Practical Example: Setting a digital weighing scale to zero before placing an object on it is a simple example of zero adjustment.

21. Power Supply and Circuit Safety

A suitable power supply is essential for reliable IoT operation. Incorrect voltage, excessive current, reversed polarity or a short circuit can damage sensors, microcontrollers and other components. Always check the datasheet or board documentation before connecting a power source.

Common Voltage Levels

Device / Board Typical Logic Level
Arduino Uno 5 V logic
ESP8266 / many NodeMCU boards 3.3 V logic
ESP32 3.3 V logic
Many Sensors Depends on the model; some support 3.3 V, 5 V or both.
Relay Module Supply and input requirements vary by module; check its specifications.

Safety Rules

  • Check the operating voltage and current requirements of every component.
  • Connect grounds together when modules require a common signal reference.
  • Use a current-limiting resistor with a bare LED.
  • Do not power motors, pumps or other high-current loads directly from a microcontroller pin.
  • Use a correctly rated driver, relay module or motor-driver circuit for loads.
  • Check polarity before connecting the supply.
  • Inspect the circuit for short circuits before switching it on.

3.3 V and 5 V Compatibility

Many ESP8266 and ESP32 boards use 3.3 V logic. Applying a 5 V signal directly to a GPIO input that is not 5 V tolerant can damage the board. Use a suitable logic-level shifter or a correctly designed voltage divider when interfacing incompatible signal levels. A board accepting 5 V at its power input does not necessarily mean its GPIO pins are 5 V tolerant.

Safety Example: Before connecting a 5 V sensor output to an ESP32 input, verify the sensor output level and the ESP32 input limits. Use level conversion when required.
Remember: Before powering an IoT circuit, check voltage, current, polarity, wiring and common-ground requirements.

22. Common IoT Circuit Troubleshooting

An IoT circuit may fail even when the program appears correct. Common causes include loose jumper wires, incorrect breadboard rows, missing ground connections, reversed component polarity, unsuitable voltage, damaged components or incorrect pin assignments. Troubleshooting should proceed step by step, starting with the simplest checks.

Common Problems and Solutions

Problem Possible Reason Solution
LED not glowing Reversed polarity, missing resistor or incorrect pin Check LED orientation, resistor, wiring and output-pin number.
Sensor gives no reading Incorrect VCC/GND connection or loose wire Verify the supply, ground and signal connections.
Random button reading Floating digital input Use an appropriate pull-up or pull-down configuration.
Board keeps restarting Weak or unstable power supply Use a stable supply rated for the board and connected load.
Wi-Fi not connecting Incorrect network details, weak signal or incompatible settings Verify SSID, password, signal strength and supported network band.
Sensor value inaccurate Incorrect wiring, unsuitable range or calibration issue Check the datasheet, wiring and calibration.

Basic Troubleshooting Steps

  1. Disconnect power before changing circuit wiring.
  2. Check the power supply and confirm that its voltage is suitable.
  3. Verify that the required grounds are connected.
  4. Compare physical pin connections with the program.
  5. Check component polarity and breadboard row connections.
  6. Use the Serial Monitor to inspect sensor readings and debug messages.
  7. Test one module at a time to isolate the faulty section.

Debug Example

Serial.begin(9600);
Serial.println(sensorValue);

Printing a sensor value to the Serial Monitor helps determine whether the controller is receiving data. If the value is missing or unexpected, inspect the sensor power, signal wire, selected pin and reading method.

Remember: Start troubleshooting with power, ground, wiring, pin numbers and polarity before changing the program.
